You see *iridescent* and know what it means. Later, describing a soap bubble, you reach for the word and come up with “shiny.”

Recognizing a word in front of you and retrieving it from a meaning are different tasks. If your goal is to use a word in writing or conversation, make room to practice the second one.

## Hide the word, keep the situation

Instead of rereading “iridescent: showing colors that change with the light or viewing angle,” try a sentence with the word missing:

> The soap bubble drifted past, its ______ surface shifting from pink to green.
>
> (adj) Showing colors that change with the light or viewing angle.

Pause and try to produce the word before revealing it. Say it or type it. Keep both the sentence and the meaning visible; together they tell you which word to recall.

## Change the example

Use a soap bubble today, a beetle’s shell another day, and a patch of oil on water later. Different examples give you a chance to ask what stays the same across them.

You can also contrast a nearby word. Something can be **shiny** because it reflects light without being **iridescent**. That extra distinction helps you decide which word actually fits.

## Come back after a gap

A correct answer while the word is still on screen does not tell you whether you can find it tomorrow. Try again after some time has passed. If recall becomes easy, lengthen the gap; if it is difficult, revisit sooner.

That is the practical idea behind spaced repetition. You can manage it yourself, use flashcards with good prompts, or choose an app that handles the schedule.

## A small routine you can repeat

1. Pick a few useful words from something you read.
2. Check their meanings and write a sentence for each.
3. Hide the words and try to fill the gaps.
4. Review later with different sentences.
5. When two words keep getting mixed up, practice what separates them.

Keep the session small enough that you will want to return. A backlog does not need to become a demand to finish everything today.
