A **WordUp alternative for vocabulary** has to solve a specific failure mode: exposure creates familiarity, but production requires recall. Seeing *obdurate* in examples can make it recognizable; producing it under a sentence constraint is a different act. The app choice is really a choice of retrieval task.

## WordUp is strongest when you want rich exposure

WordUp’s public pitch is coherent: learn English words through real-world context, including examples from media and everyday usage, with review layered on top. That is a real strength. If your main problem is that dictionary definitions feel dead, [WordUp](https://www.wordupapp.co/) attacks the right weakness.

Video context is especially useful for advanced ESL learners because usage is not only semantic. Register, tone, and collocation matter. A word can be technically correct and still sound theatrical, bureaucratic, childish, clinical, or archaic. Seeing the word in varied contexts helps prevent the sterile “definition only” version of vocabulary learning.

The tradeoff is the retrieval demand. Much of the value is still *recognition*: you see or hear the word, then connect it to meaning. Recognition is not fake learning, but it is not the same as being able to summon the word when a sentence needs it. If you recognize a word but cannot produce it, the missing training is not more exposure. It is constrained recall.

That is the split. WordUp is a strong fit when you want richer encounters with words. Lexi is the better fit when the missing behavior is active recall in context.

## A WordUp alternative for vocabulary should force recall, not recognition

Lexi teaches vocabulary through spaced repetition delivered as dynamically generated crossword puzzles. The clue is not “define this word.” It is a fill-in-the-blank sentence plus a meaning-evoking hint, so the act you practice is closer to actual language use: a context demands a word, and you have to produce it.

That matters because definitions are easy to overfit to. If a flashcard always says “stubbornly refusing to change one’s opinion,” you may learn the rhythm of that definition instead of the word. Lexi cycles each word through multiple differently phrased clues — currently 3 per word — under one spaced-repetition schedule per word. Different surface forms, one underlying item. There is no single prompt to memorize.

Every unseen word gets a word-card introduction before the puzzle. You are never quizzed cold on a word you could not possibly know. The card gives a definition, an image designed to evoke the meaning, an example sentence matching the image, and connotation tags.

For example, *cerebral* means “involving intelligence rather than emotions or instinct.” Its card sentence is: “The debate was a cerebral exercise, focusing on logic and evidence rather than emotional appeals.”

Then the puzzle tests whether the word can be recovered from context. That sequence is deliberate: introduce meaning first, require recall second, schedule the next encounter from performance.

## The schedule should belong to the word, not the flashcard

Spaced repetition is review at expanding intervals, with harder items returning sooner. The common failure is making the learner manage the schedule manually: flip a card, judge yourself, press Again/Hard/Good/Easy, repeat until review becomes homework.

Lexi removes that decision. SRS grading is inferred automatically from solve timing — fast, normal, or slow — normalized for clue and word length. If you solve quickly, the word can move outward. If you hesitate, it comes back sooner. No self-grading ritual.

This is also why crosswords are not decoration. They turn review into a production task with constraints. The grid gives letter intersections; the clue gives semantic context; the answer still has to be retrieved. That combination is harder to fake than tapping a definition you half-recognize.

## Comparison: WordUp, Vocabulary.com, Anki, and Lexi

| Tool | Core mechanism | Best fit | Main tradeoff for retention |
|---|---|---|---|
| [WordUp](https://www.wordupapp.co/) | Rich word exposure through context, examples, and review | Learners who want words to feel less abstract | Exposure can improve familiarity without forcing production |
| [Vocabulary.com](https://www.vocabulary.com/) | Dictionary and quiz workflow around word meanings | Learners who like quick definition-centered practice | Definition recognition can outrun usable recall |
| [Anki](https://docs.ankiweb.net/getting-started.html) | User-managed flashcards with spaced repetition | Arbitrary self-made decks: anatomy, kanji, facts | Vocabulary quality depends on the prompts you create; fixed cards are easy to overfit |
| Lexi | Dynamically generated crossword puzzles with contextual clues and automatic SRS grading | Learners who want recall practice without flashcard review homework | English only, iOS only; one sense per word so far |

Anki is the right tool when the learning object is arbitrary and self-authored. If you are memorizing anatomy, flags, kanji, or a custom professional deck, the blank-card model is the point. For vocabulary, the card itself becomes the bottleneck: one prompt, one answer, one phrasing. Lexi makes the prompt variable because the word, not the card, is the unit being learned.

## Who Lexi is for

Use Lexi if you are the kind of learner who already reads, notices unknown words, and wants a system that converts noticing into usable vocabulary.

It is especially aligned if:

- flashcard queues make vocabulary feel like administrative work;
- you remember seeing a word but cannot summon it while writing or speaking;
- you want contextual fill-in-the-blank recall, not another feed;
- you care about synonym nuance — Lexi lets you tap any two synonyms in a synset to see precisely how they differ;
- you want a free iPhone/iPad vocabulary app with no ads at all.

The dataset has 20,000 words ordered roughly by rarity for maximal real-world utility per unit time spent; about 3,000 currently have pipeline-grade clues, and the first about 1,500 have illustrated word cards. Definitions, clues, and images are maintained through regular updates from in-app feedback, not frozen as a static deck.

Do not bother if you need Android, non-English vocabulary, or full polysemy coverage today.
