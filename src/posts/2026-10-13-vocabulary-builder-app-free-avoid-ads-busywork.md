A free vocabulary app can still cost you attention. The usual failure mode is not price; it is a review loop shaped around taps, interruptions, streak anxiety, or subscription pressure instead of durable recall. If you searched for “vocabulary builder app free,” judge the app by what happens *after* install.

The useful question is not whether the download button says $0. The useful question is whether the free tier contains the actual learning system, or only a sample of it.

## A free tier matters only if the review loop stays intact

A vocabulary app has one job: make words available when you need them. That requires retrieval practice — pulling the word from memory — and spaced repetition, the scheduling of reviews at widening intervals when recall succeeds.

A free tier is fake if it withholds either part. A few daily words, a blocked review queue, or interruptions around definitions create the wrong incentive: you stop when the app asks you to manage the app, not when your memory has done the work.

Lexi’s free tier keeps the core loop intact on iPhone and iPad: no ads at all, no ad-removal purchase, and vocabulary practice through spaced repetition delivered as dynamically generated crossword puzzles. Premium exists, but it is not ransom for silence. Premium adds contrastive training and every companion at $4.99/month, $11.99/quarter, or $35.99/year.

## Ads break recall at the worst possible moment

Ads are tolerable in passive browsing. They are corrosive in retrieval.

When you are trying to produce a word from a clue, you are holding a partial semantic target in working memory: the situation, the tone, the length pattern, maybe the first letter. Interrupt that with an ad and the app has inserted a context switch at the exact point where the memory trace should be strengthened.

That is why “free with ads” is not equivalent to free. The app may cost no money, but the payment is attention at the moment attention is doing the work.

Lexi has no ads. The free version does not need an ad-removal tier because there is nothing to remove.

## Review friction compounds faster than price

Manual flashcard grading looks harmless: Again, Hard, Good, Easy. In vocabulary, those buttons add a second task after every recall attempt: now you must grade your own memory while deciding whether hesitation, typo, or partial recall counts.

I built Lexi to remove that decision. SRS grading is inferred automatically from solve timing — fast, normal, or slow — normalized for clue and word length. You solve; the scheduler updates. No self-grading ritual.

The same design choice explains why Lexi uses crossword clues instead of definition cards. Clues are fill-in-the-blank sentences plus a meaning-evoking hint, so recall happens in context. Every word cycles through multiple differently phrased clues — currently three per word — under one spaced-repetition schedule per word. Different surface forms, one underlying item. No fixed phrasing to overfit to.

## Word quality matters more than word-count theater

A large word list is cheap. A useful word list is ordered, introduced, and reviewed.

Lexi’s dataset contains 20,000 words ordered roughly by rarity for maximal real-world utility per unit time spent. About 3,000 words currently have pipeline-grade clues. The first ~1,500 words have illustrated word cards.

A card is not decoration. It gives you a first encounter before recall begins: image, definition, example sentence, and connotation tags.

For *anomaly*, the card definition is: “A deviation from a rule or from what is regarded as normal; an outlier.” The example sentence anchors the sense: “The sudden drop in temperature was an anomaly in the otherwise warm summer.”
